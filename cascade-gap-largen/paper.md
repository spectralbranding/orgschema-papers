# A Large-N Confirmatory Necessary-Condition Test of Closing-Time Structural Gaps in Acquisition Integration Failure

Dmitry Zharnikov

ORCID: 0009-0000-6893-9231

Concept DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.21755969](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21755969)

Working Paper v1.0.0 — August 2026

## Abstract

Post-acquisition integration outcomes remain hard to predict at signing: meta-analysis attributes their variance to unidentified moderators, yet the field lacks a structural, pre-close, checkable predictor. This study tests, confirmatorily and at large N, whether a closing-time *cascade gap* — a product–process or process–organization mismatch created when an asset or process crosses the ownership boundary without its adjacent producing or governing tier transferring alongside it — is a *necessary* condition for a cascade-type integration failure realized over three to five years. On a case-control sample of 350 completed transactions drawn on deal structure and coded from disjoint evidence by rotated raters, the necessary-condition relationship is supported only in a bounded form: conditional on no detected gap, the cascade-type failure rate is low (.073, exact upper 95% bound .112) yet not zero, and only about half of failures carried a gap (necessity consistency .517). Structural conformance therefore functions as a bounded safe harbor and a pre-close negative screen for one failure mode, not as a strong necessary condition or a general predictor of deal success. The separated-measurement and structure-based sampling design is a contribution alongside the estimate, and the sampling frame's rate of announced-but-unconsummated bids is a reportable methodological finding.

**Keywords**: mergers and acquisitions; post-merger integration; necessary condition analysis; case-control design; inter-rater reliability; structural gap; safe harbor; large language model coding

---

Whether an acquisition will integrate well is, for the most part, judged after the fact. Decades of research establish that post-acquisition integration outcomes are a first-order determinant of whether a deal creates or destroys value, yet the same literature concludes that the drivers are incompletely identified: a meta-analysis of post-acquisition performance attributes much of the outstanding variance to unidentified moderators [@king-2004-metaanalyses-postacquisition-performance], and successive reviews of the field describe integration as the stage where value is most often lost without a consensus structural account of why [@haleblian-2009-taking-stock-what; @cartwright-2006-thirty-years-mergers; @graebner-2017-process-postmerger-integration]. The empirical regularity that integration failure concentrates at the boundary between operational processes and the organization that runs them [@larsson-1999-integrating-strategic-organizational] points at structure, but the field has no pre-close, checkable predictor of a specific integration-failure mode — one that operates on a fact known at signing rather than on a management judgment formed only after the deal has closed.

This paper tests a structural candidate for such a predictor. A *cascade gap* is a mismatch created at deal closing when a customer-facing product or asset, or an operational process, crosses the ownership boundary without the adjacent tier that produces or governs it transferring or aligning coherently alongside it. The construct specializes a six-tier ontology of the acquisition target, in which a firm decomposes into ordered tiers from owner intent through business model, legal entity, product, process, and organization, and in which failures propagate along the service hierarchy that binds those tiers [@zharnikov-2026-dual-hierarchies-organizational-transferability-six]. A companion formal treatment develops the compositional grammar under which a transaction's transfer content can leave such a gap; the present paper does not rest its hypothesis on that treatment, but states and tests the empirical claim self-contained. The hypothesis under test is deliberately scoped as a *necessary-condition and safe-harbor* claim rather than a sufficiency one: a cascade gap makes a cascade-type failure possible, not certain, and its absence should mark a deal as structurally safe on this particular failure mode.

The study contributes on three fronts. First, it supplies the first large-N *confirmatory* necessary-condition test of the structural-gap hypothesis, moving it from a small, exploratory, post-hoc reading to a pre-registered test on a sample built to contain the at-risk structural tail. Second, it advances a measurement-and-sampling design that closes two threats a small pilot exposed: the gap and the outcome are coded from disjoint evidence by rotated raters to remove construct porosity, and the sample is drawn on deal structure — never on outcome — to populate the tail a random deal frame under-samples. Third, it reports the safe-harbor result as a practical pre-close negative screen and, in doing so, surfaces a methodological finding about archival deal sampling: full-text search on distressed-deal filings over-includes announced-but-unconsummated bids at a rate worth reporting in its own right. The headline result is honest rather than triumphal: at large N the safe harbor is real but bounded, which both corrects the earlier small-sample reading and sharpens what the screen can and cannot promise.

## Theoretical Background and Hypothesis

### *The structural-gap hypothesis*

The six-tier ontology the cascade gap specializes treats an acquisition target as an ordered stack of tiers bound by a service hierarchy: each tier depends on the tier below it for its productive substrate and on the tier above it for its governing constraint [@zharnikov-2026-dual-hierarchies-organizational-transferability-six]. In this reading, an acquisition is not a monolithic transfer of a firm but a bundle of tier-level transfers, and integration risk arises when the bundle is incomplete in a structurally consequential way — when a tier crosses the ownership boundary while the adjacent tier that produces or governs it does not. Two such mismatches define the cascade gap operationally: a product–process gap, in which a customer-facing product or asset transfers without the operational process that produces it, and a process–organization gap, in which a process transfers without the organizational unit that runs it. Both are visible in the closing-time deal documents, because both are properties of the transaction's transfer content rather than of its later management. Concretely: when a branded product line is acquired but the plants, workflows, or supplier routines that produce it remain with the seller, the closing structure leaves a product–process gap; when a process is acquired but the team, reporting lines, and decision rights that execute it are not, it leaves a process–organization gap. In each case the transaction severs, at signing, a dependency that day-to-day operation requires.

This framing is a modularity argument applied to the transaction event. Modular-systems theory holds that a system decomposes cleanly only along interfaces where the coupling between components is low, and that recombining modules across a poorly chosen interface imports hidden dependencies [@baldwin-2000-design-rules-power; @schilling-2000-toward-general-modular]. A cascade gap is precisely a recombination across a high-coupling tier interface: the transaction severs a dependency the service hierarchy says cannot be severed without loss of continuity. The mechanism is adjacent to, but distinct from, capability-gap accounts of renewal, in which internal frictions and missing capabilities impede the redeployment of resources after a boundary change [@capron-2009-selection-capability-how]; the cascade gap locates the risk in the *structure of what was transferred at closing*, a fact fixed before any post-close management begins.

This structural locus also distinguishes the cascade gap from the post-merger-integration literature on integration *structure*, which studies the acquirer's choice of whether and how deeply to structurally integrate the target after the deal closes, and when that choice helps or harms the retention and leverage of acquired capabilities [@puranam-2009-integrating-acquired-capabilities; @puranam-2007-what-they-know]. That work takes the transaction as given and asks how to manage it; the cascade gap is a property of the transaction itself — which tiers crossed the boundary and which did not — and is therefore fixed before the integration-structure decision is made. The two are complementary: a cascade gap constrains what the later integration choice can repair, because a tier left behind at closing cannot be structurally integrated afterward.

### *A necessary-condition, not a sufficiency, claim*

The hypothesis is scoped as a necessary condition. A cascade-type integration failure — a realized disruption located specifically at the product–process or process–organization interface over the three-to-five-year horizon — should not occur without a cascade gap having been present at closing; equivalently, cascade-conformance (coherent joint transfer of product, process, and organization) should function as a structural safe harbor against this failure mode. The claim is not that a gap causes failure: a gap makes the failure *possible*, and many gapped deals will nonetheless integrate successfully for reasons the structure does not capture. Scoping the claim this way matters both theoretically and statistically. Theoretically, it matches the mechanism: the service hierarchy tells us which severed dependencies *can* disrupt integration, not which ones *will*. Statistically, a necessary-condition claim is evaluated on the conformant cell — the rate of failure among deals with no gap — and is tested with Necessary Condition Analysis, a method purpose-built for "X is necessary for Y" structures with its own ceiling logic and effect size [@dul-2016-necessary-condition-analysis], rather than with a regression coefficient that answers the sufficiency question the data are poorly placed to address.

The necessary-condition reading is also the correctly-scoped confirmatory hypothesis for this study specifically. An earlier, small, exploratory pass on a handful of documented deals suggested a *perfect* safe harbor; that reading was post-hoc and, on so few conformant cases, fragile to a single reclassification. The present study pre-registers the necessary-condition and safe-harbor claim as its confirmatory hypothesis and tests it on a sample large enough that the conformant cell can be estimated with a tight interval and can survive individual reclassifications — the condition the small sample could not meet.

## Method

### *Sample and case-control design*

The sample is a case-control design drawn on deal *structure*. From a corpus of completed, material US transactions (closing 2006–2018, transaction value at least one billion US dollars), the study over-samples four gap-prone structural strata — carve-outs, joint ventures, roll-ups, and distressed asset deals — against matched going-concern controls in which product, process, and organization transfer together. The final sample is $N = 350$: 175 gap-prone deals (44 carve-outs, 44 joint ventures, 44 roll-ups, 43 distressed asset deals) and 175 controls matched on industry, size band, and era. The sampling variable is the deal type, a closing-time structural property; the design never selects on the outcome. Inclusion was pre-registered and executed as a seeded, reproducible draw with a build-time gate that logged every included and replaced case, so the frame is fixed and outcome-blind before any coding. Matched controls support the secondary outcome model rather than the primary necessary-condition test, following standard matched-design logic [@rosenbaum-1983-central-role-propensity].

Each transaction yields two disjoint sub-dossiers built separately from the start. The *structural* sub-dossier is assembled only from closing-time deal documents — the merger agreement, registration statement, proxy, and closing-era current reports — and contains no post-deal language. The *outcome* sub-dossier is assembled only from the three-to-five-year subsequent record — later annual filings and reputable contemporaneous press — and contains no deal-structure language. Building the two separately from the start, rather than slicing one combined dossier, is deliberate: it fixes the transaction whose window defines the outcome, so that a later, downstream event is never mis-attributed to the coded deal's structure.

### *Construct-validity separation and coding*

Coding enforces construct-validity separation. The structural gap and the realized outcome are coded in two passes, each from its own sub-dossier only. Four frontier reasoning models served as blinded raters — Claude (Opus 4.8), Gemini (3.1 Pro), Grok (4.5), and GPT (5.6) — coding from an evidentiary dossier and returning a structured judgment. Raters never saw the hypothesis, the predicted direction, or a case's gap-prone-versus-control status. Crucially, the pass that codes the gap for a given case and the pass that codes that case's outcome are assigned to *different* models under a pre-registered three-of-four rotation, so no single model's idiosyncrasy contributes shared-method variance to both the independent and the dependent variable. Every model call is logged in full — prompt, parameters, and response — and the coding harness, rotation map, coded dataset, and call logs are released with the paper. Language models coding from a dossier are treated here as a measurement instrument whose reliability is estimated per construct; reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity, a limitation returned to below. When the post-deal record yielded no determinable outcome, the case was coded UNCERTAIN and set aside rather than counted as no-failure, so that absence of evidence never became evidence of no failure.

### *Reliability estimation*

Inter-rater reliability is estimated per construct across the three rater slots each case receives. The study reports both Fleiss' $\kappa$ [@fleiss-1971-measuring-nominal-scale] and Gwet's $AC_1$ [@gwet-2008-computing-interrater-reliability], and pre-registers $AC_1$ as the primary reliability read. The reason is the outcome construct's rarity: cascade-type failure is a tail event, so raw agreement is high but the marginals are skewed, the condition under which $\kappa$ is known to be deflated toward zero — the prevalence paradox. $AC_1$ is designed to remain stable under high agreement with skewed marginals and is therefore the appropriate primary coefficient for the rare cell; $\kappa$ is reported alongside it for transparency, not as the headline.

### *Analysis plan and identification*

The primary analysis is Necessary Condition Analysis for the dichotomous case [@dul-2016-necessary-condition-analysis]. The pre-registered confirmatory quantity is the conditional failure rate in the no-gap cell, $P(\text{fail} \mid \text{no gap})$, reported with an exact Clopper–Pearson upper bound — the empty-cell / safe-harbor criterion. Necessity consistency, $P(\text{gap} \mid \text{fail})$, is reported as the share of failures that carried a gap. The 2×2 is formed only over cases with a determinable outcome; outcome-uncertain cases are set aside first. Sufficiency, $P(\text{fail} \mid \text{gap})$, is reported for completeness but is **not** headlined: a sample selected on structure over-represents gapped deals and can inflate the conditional failure rate given a gap, so only the necessary-condition and safe-harbor direction is interpreted as confirmatory. This is the study's central identification safeguard. The design is observational and warrants a necessity-and-safe-harbor reading, not an average causal effect; because the independent variable is fixed at closing and the dependent variable realizes over the subsequent three to five years, reverse causation is minimal, but no randomization identifies a treatment effect. A secondary outcome model — logistic regression and a discrete-time survival specification with industry, size, era, relatedness, and acquirer-experience controls [@allison-1982-discretetime-methods-analysis] — is held to a robustness role, subordinate to the primary test.

## Results

### *Inter-rater reliability*

Structural-gap coding is reliable at the level content analysis expects: Gwet's $AC_1 = .789$ (Fleiss' $\kappa = .514$). Outcome-pathway coding shows the signature of the prevalence paradox — a large gap between a very low $\kappa = .069$ and a moderate $AC_1 = .488$. Because the failure pathway is rare, raters agree on the great majority of cases (raw agreement is high), yet $\kappa$'s chance-correction, driven by the near-degenerate marginals, collapses toward zero. This divergence is exactly the condition the design anticipated in pre-registering $AC_1$ as the primary reliability read; the moderate $AC_1$ is the honest reliability of a rare-event coding task, and the low $\kappa$ is an artifact of prevalence, not evidence of unreliable coding. ### *The confirmatory necessary-condition test*

Of the 350 coded cases, 55 were set aside as outcome-uncertain, leaving 295 with a determinable outcome for the confirmatory 2×2. The contingency of the structural gap against the realized cascade-type failure is: 15 gap-and-failure, 88 gap-and-no-failure, 14 no-gap-and-failure, and 178 no-gap-and-no-failure (Table 2).

The pre-registered safe-harbor quantity is the failure rate conditional on no gap: $P(\text{fail} \mid \text{no gap}) = .073$, with an exact Clopper–Pearson upper 95% bound of $.112$. This is low — a deal with no coded structural gap fails at the product–process or process–organization interface roughly seven times in a hundred — but it is not zero: 14 cases failed with no coded gap, so the safe-harbor cell is not empty. Necessity consistency is $P(\text{gap} \mid \text{fail}) = .517$: only about half of cascade-type failures carried a detectable structural gap. The gap–outcome association is small in magnitude ($\varphi = .116$; odds ratio $= 2.17$; risk difference $P(\text{fail} \mid \text{gap}) - P(\text{fail} \mid \text{no gap}) = .073$).

The reading is that the necessary-condition relationship is real but weaker than the earlier exploratory reading predicted. Under conventional necessary-condition thresholds — a consistency near or above .90, or an empty ceiling zone in the dichotomous case — strong necessity is not supported: the ceiling zone is not empty (14 no-gap failures populate it), so the Necessary Condition Analysis ceiling effect size falls below its maximum, consistent with the small association ($\varphi = .116$). What is supported is a bounded safe harbor, not a strong necessary condition. At small N the safe harbor appeared perfect; at $N = 350$, with the conformant cell estimated on 192 determinable no-gap cases, it is bounded rather than absolute. The 14 no-gap failures and the roughly even-odds necessity consistency together mean the large-N test *bounds* the safe harbor — it establishes a low, tightly-estimated residual failure rate under conformance — rather than confirming a strong necessary condition. This corrects the perfect-safe-harbor artifact of the small sample, and it does so in the direction that clean, separated measurement predicts: removing construct porosity should, and here does, weaken an over-strong margin.

### *Sampling-frame precision*

A methodological finding emerged from the distressed stratum. Full-text search on distressed-deal filings over-includes announced-but-unconsummated bids: 8 of the 43 distressed cases (about 19%) were terminated, expired, or lost bids in which no ownership boundary was ever crossed. Such a bid is a non-event for this study — with no completed transfer, there is no closing structure to code and no realized integration outcome — not missing data. Combined with the rule that an absent post-deal record is coded UNCERTAIN rather than no-failure, this handling set aside 55 of the 350 cases as outcome-uncertain, ensuring that neither non-consummation nor absence of evidence ever filled the no-failure cell that the safe-harbor test depends on. The 19% non-consummation rate in structure-based distressed sampling is a reportable characteristic of archival deal frames in its own right, useful to any study that draws deals from full-text filing search.

### *Robustness and companion computation script*

A secondary outcome model is specified as a robustness check rather than a headline result. Because the sample is selected on structure, an average-effect estimate is confounded by design in the sufficiency direction; the primary necessary-condition test is the interpretively clean quantity, and a logistic and discrete-time survival specification with industry, size, era, relatedness, and acquirer-experience controls [@allison-1982-discretetime-methods-analysis] is provided in the released pipeline to show the direction and stability of the association, not to license a causal effect size. The association is small ($\varphi = .116$, odds ratio $2.17$) and consistent with the necessary-but-not-sufficient reading: within this structure-selected sample a gap is associated with roughly twice the odds of a cascade-type failure while leaving most gapped deals successful — a within-sample association that, given the selection on structure, must not be read as a general risk multiplier.

*Companion computation script.* All reported quantities are reproduced by the released analysis pipeline. The confirmatory 2×2, the exact Clopper–Pearson bound, necessity consistency, and the sufficiency figure are produced by `analyze_full_draw.py`; the registered sample size is derived by `power_analysis_s5.py` (seed 20260729); the coded dataset is produced by the coding runner `full_draw_code.py` under the pre-registered rotation (seed 20260729). Each script carries its run command in its docstring and a `README.md` in the code directory documenting what it reproduces; running the pipeline on the released dataset reproduces every figure cited above. The scripts, the coded dataset (350 cases), the 700 per-case coded records, and the full model-call logs are published in the paper's public code and data repository (see Data and Code Availability).

## Discussion

### *A bounded safe harbor*

The study set out to test whether a closing-time cascade gap is necessary for a cascade-type integration failure. The answer is a qualified yes. Conformance to coherent joint transfer at closing is associated with a low failure rate at the product–process and process–organization interfaces — a conditional rate of $.073$ with a tight exact upper bound of $.112$ — which is decision-relevant information available at signing. But the safe harbor is bounded, not perfect: some deals fail at these interfaces without a detectable structural gap, and only about half of failures carried one. Two readings of the residual are worth distinguishing. The first is substantive: the service hierarchy identifies a *sufficient set* of structurally severable dependencies but not the *complete set*, so some cascade-type failures arise from couplings the six-tier decomposition does not resolve — a boundary of the theory, and a target for refinement. The second is measurement: a rare outcome coded from an archival record carries irreducible error, and some no-gap failures may be gapped deals whose structural mismatch the closing documents did not disclose. The design cannot fully separate these, and both point to the same conclusion — the strong "no gap ⇒ no failure" claim is not supported at scale, and the correctly-scoped claim is the bounded one.

This matters as a correction. The exploratory small-sample reading suggested a perfect safe harbor; a confirmatory large-N test with separated measurement finds a bounded one. That is the normal and desirable trajectory of a claim moving from a suggestive pattern to a tested estimate, and it illustrates why necessary-condition claims in strategy need samples built to contain the at-risk tail with decoupled measurement: without them, a perfect-looking margin can be an artifact of a small conformant cell and porous coding.

### *A pre-close negative screen*

Even bounded, the safe harbor supports a practical deliverable. Because the cascade gap is a property of the transaction's transfer content, it is knowable at signing, before integration begins. The result licenses a pre-close negative screen: a deal with no structural gap is low-risk for this specific failure mode, at a residual rate of $.073$ with a tight upper bound of $.112$, rather than risk-free. This is a different and more honest managerial claim than a predictive score. It does not tell an acquirer that a gapped deal will fail — most do not — but it tells them that the absence of a gap is genuine, if bounded, structural reassurance on one well-defined failure mode, computed from facts fixed at the term sheet rather than from a post-hoc integration judgment. It also directs due-diligence attention: because a gap roughly doubles the odds of interface failure, a detected gap is a specific, structurally-located flag for integration planning, not a generic risk marker.

### *Boundary conditions and limitations*

The study applies to single, completed, material transactions in the US public-filing corpus over 2006–2018, and to cascade-type failures specifically — disruption at the product–process or process–organization interface over three to five years — not to all M&A failure and not to within-firm reorganization. Within that scope, several limitations bound the estimate. The design is observational; it warrants a necessity-and-safe-harbor reading, not an average causal effect, though the fixed-at-closing independent variable and the later-realized dependent variable make reverse causation unlikely. The raters are language models: rotation and blinding limit but do not eliminate shared-method bias, and reliability is not validity, so a construct-validity study of the outcome coder against human coding on a held-out subsample is an important next step. Outcome measurement is subject to survivorship and disclosure gaps in the archival record, mitigated but not removed by the outcome-uncertain set-aside rule. Finally, the discriminating structural tail is partly concentrated in the strata the underlying theory was developed against, so generalization beyond the four sampled gap-prone strata to the full deal-type space is not established here. These bound, rather than overturn, the central finding: a low, tightly-estimated, non-zero residual failure rate under structural conformance.

### *Pre-registration and deviations*

The necessary-condition and safe-harbor hypothesis, the case-control structural sampling, the separated two-pass coding with per-construct rotation, the $AC_1$-as-primary reliability read, the outcome-uncertain set-aside rule, and the sufficiency-not-headlined constraint were all pre-registered before any datum was coded. The registered sample size was fixed by a power analysis targeting the width of the exact upper bound on the safe-harbor rate. The confirmatory frame was fixed in advance and is reported as run; the study makes no post-hoc changes to the hypothesis, the coding scheme, or the analysis. The substantive result — that the safe harbor is bounded rather than perfect — is a finding within the registered design, not a deviation from it.
